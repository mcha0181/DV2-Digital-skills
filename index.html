Australia is going digital. Not everyone can keep up.

The national score is rising, but older Australians and people with less formal education still face greater barriers. Follow the evidence from people, to places, to everyday opportunities.

47.4index points separate Australians aged 18–34 and people aged 75 or older in 2025. This is a score gap, not a percentage.
01 / People

Progress is real. So is the gap.

Digital ability increased between 2023 and 2025. Compare the national picture with older adults and people who did not finish secondary school, then look at the size of the remaining differences.

The score for people aged 75 and over rose from 23.3 to 41.5 between 2023 and 2025. That is progress, but the gap with younger adults remains large.

02 / Beyond skills

A connection is only part of the story.

Access, affordability and digital ability are related, but they are not interchangeable. The Census adds another view: how people chose to return their forms.

A note on the two datasetsThe Index measures digital inclusion, while the Census charts measure online form submission. Filling in a Census form online is not, by itself, a test of digital literacy. The 2025 affordability score also uses a changed definition and should not be compared directly with earlier years.
03 / Around Australia

One country, different digital experiences.

Three map types answer separate questions: compare dimensions of inclusion as equal-sized tiles, examine online Census submission on a geographic symbol map, and compare actual state boundaries with a continuous digital-ability choropleth. Rankings beside the maps reveal differences that can be hard to judge from colour and circle area alone.

Online Census participation and digital ability are different indicators. Their rankings are shown separately; neither proves an individual person has particular skills.

04 / Education and work

Digital exclusion has practical consequences.

The divide is not just about age or postcode. In the 2025 Index, digital ability among employed Australians was 17.6 index points higher than among people outside the labour force. The comparison describes an association, not an effect of employment.

In 2025, 66.5% of Australians aged 75+ were classified as digitally excluded or highly excluded, compared with 20.6% of Australians overall. Education matters too: the comparable figure was 54.5% for people who did not complete secondary school.

What this can mean in daily lifeStudy materials, job applications and training now often sit behind a screen. These group differences can suggest barriers to online services; they do not show that digital skills caused any individual employment or education outcome.
